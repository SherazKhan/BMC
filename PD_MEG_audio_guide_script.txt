Welcome to this audio guide on magnetoencephalography in Parkinson's disease. Over the next fifteen minutes, we will walk through eight research papers. We'll start with a primer on the technique, then follow the story chronologically, from early spectral findings, through dementia and treatment effects, to connectivity, task studies, neurochemistry, and finally a new review on clinical translation.

Let's begin with the technique itself.

Part one. The primer. Magnetoencephalography, or MEG, in neurodegenerative disorders. This is a review by C. J. Stam.

Stam opens with a simple point. The mechanisms that produce clinical symptoms in Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease are still incompletely understood. MEG is a relatively new functional neuroimaging technique. It records the magnetic fields generated by the brain's own activity, using large arrays of sensors that cover the whole head, and it does this simultaneously across all of them.

What has MEG shown so far? First, that both Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease show characteristic patterns of abnormal oscillatory activity, in different frequency bands. Second, that studies of distributed functional networks, in other words, how brain regions talk to each other, reveal distinct patterns of abnormal connectivity. These patterns differ between demented and non-demented Parkinson's patients, and differ again in Alzheimer's disease.

Stam also notes that in Parkinson's, abnormal oscillatory activity and disturbed connectivity may respond differently to dopaminergic treatment. That is a theme that will return throughout this guide: what you measure depends on medication state.

Looking ahead, he suggests that the field could benefit from new technology, such as ultra low field MRI, and from a well-defined theoretical framework. His example is graph theory, a mathematical approach for describing brain networks. Keep that in mind, because several later papers rely on network ideas.

So the take-home from part one: MEG gives a window onto both local oscillations and network connectivity, and both are altered in Parkinson's disease.

Part two. One of the earliest studies. Low-frequency MEG activity and MRI evaluation in Parkinson's disease, by Kotini and colleagues.

The background here is that low-frequency activity had already been reported in Parkinson's disease, and the authors wanted to look at MEG recordings in the frequency domain, that is, by breaking the signal into its component frequencies.

The methods were modest by today's standards. Recordings were made in a magnetically shielded room, using a whole-head, one hundred and twenty-two channel gradiometer. Nine patients with Parkinson's disease took part, along with nine age and gender matched healthy volunteers. MRI, with T1 and T2 weighted images, was available from the patients' records.

The result: prominent low frequencies could be seen in the spectrum of the Parkinson's patients. In the control group, the frequency range was around six hertz in the majority of channels. And the MRI, notably, did not reveal specific findings in any case. In other words, the structural scan looked unremarkable, while the MEG spectrum did show a difference.

The conclusion was cautious. MEG could be a complementary method in the diagnostic evaluation of Parkinson's disease, using the spatial distribution of the raw data in the frequency domain. With only nine patients per group, this is a pilot-scale study, but it planted the idea of MEG as a diagnostic aid, and that idea is revisited at the end of this guide.

Part three. Resting state oscillatory brain dynamics in Parkinson's disease, an MEG study, by Bosboom and colleagues, from 2006.

The question was this. The pathophysiological mechanisms of cognitive dysfunction and dementia in Parkinson's are still poorly understood. Altered resting state oscillatory brain activity may reflect underlying neuropathological changes. So the team compared three groups: thirteen Parkinson's patients with dementia, thirteen without dementia, and thirteen healthy elderly controls.

Whole head MEG was recorded in the morning, in two conditions: eyes closed and eyes open. The team calculated relative spectral power, using a Fast Fourier Transformation, in the delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma bands.

Here are the results. In the non-demented Parkinson's patients, relative theta power was diffusely increased, while beta power was concomitantly decreased, compared with controls. Gamma power was decreased in central and parietal channels. So already, before any dementia, the spectrum is shifted toward slower frequencies.

In the demented patients, there was a further change. Compared with the non-demented group, they showed a diffuse increase in relative delta power, and to a lesser extent theta power, together with a decrease in relative alpha, beta, and to a lesser extent gamma power. And reactivity to eye opening, the normal drop in alpha activity when you open your eyes, was much reduced in the demented group.

The conclusion: Parkinson's disease is characterized by a slowing of resting state brain activity, involving theta, beta and gamma bands. Dementia in Parkinson's is associated with a further slowing, additionally involving delta and alpha bands, plus reduced reactivity to eye opening.

The significance, according to the authors, is that these differing patterns suggest that dementia in Parkinson's involves additional mechanisms, on top of the progression of the pathological changes already present in non-demented patients. Think of it as a staircase of slowing: first theta up and beta down, then delta up and alpha down.

Part four. Cholinergic modulation of MEG resting state oscillatory activity in Parkinson's disease related dementia, again by Bosboom and colleagues, in 2009.

This is a natural follow-up. Since earlier EEG and MEG work had shown a slowing of resting state oscillatory activity in Parkinson's disease dementia, the aim here was to test whether the cholinesterase inhibitor rivastigmine would reverse that slowing.

Eight patients with Parkinson's disease dementia had whole head MEG recorded at rest, before and after treatment with rivastigmine. Relative spectral power was calculated in the five classic frequency bands, over fronto-central, parieto-occipital and temporal regions.

After treatment, patients showed an increase in relative alpha power in parieto-occipital and temporal regions, together with a diffuse increase in beta power. Delta power decreased in fronto-central and parieto-occipital regions. So the spectrum shifted away from slow activity, and toward faster activity.

The authors concluded that rivastigmine at least partly counteracts the slowing of resting state brain activity known to occur in Parkinson's disease dementia. They argue this emphasizes the prominent role of degeneration of the cholinergic system in the pathophysiology of dementia in Parkinson's. And looking forward, they suggest MEG might help select the patients who may optimally benefit from cholinergic treatment. This is an early example of MEG as a pharmacodynamic marker, a way of seeing a drug's effect on brain activity. Remember it, because acetylcholine reappears in the neurochemistry paper later on.

Part five. Resting state functional connectivity as a marker of disease progression in Parkinson's disease, a longitudinal MEG study, by Olde Dubbelink and colleagues, published in 2013.

So far we've looked at power in each frequency band. This study asks about connectivity, meaning how synchronized different brain regions are, and, importantly, it follows patients over time.

Using a source-space approach, with detailed anatomical mapping, the team assessed functional connectivity for temporal, prefrontal and high order sensory association areas. These are regions known to show neuropathological changes in the early clinical stages of the disease.

At baseline, early stage, untreated patients, twelve of them, had lower parahippocampal and temporal delta band connectivity, and higher temporal alpha one band connectivity, compared with controls.

Then came the longitudinal analysis, in a larger group of forty-three patients, over a four year period. It revealed decreases in alpha one and alpha two band connectivity, across multiple seed regions, and these decreases were associated with motor or cognitive deterioration.

The authors summarize the arc like this. In the earliest clinical stages, delta and alpha one connectivity are altered in temporal cortical regions. With progression, there is a reversal of the initial alpha one changes, plus additional decreases in alpha two connectivity, evolving into a more widespread cortical pattern. Early on, alpha one connectivity is higher than normal; later, it falls. These changes appear to reflect clinically relevant phenomena, and so hold promise as a marker of disease progression, with potential predictive value for clinical outcome.

That is the key message of this paper, and arguably of the whole field: connectivity changes track how patients get worse.

Part six. Auditory entrainment of motor responses in older adults with and without Parkinson's disease, an MEG study, by Buard and colleagues.

Now we shift from rest to task. The background: medical therapies for Parkinson's have advanced tremendously since the nineteen sixties. Behavioral therapies, such as rhythmic auditory stimulation, or RAS, have been developed more recently, and have demonstrated efficacy. But the neural mechanisms of RAS are only vaguely understood.

So the team examined the neurophysiology of RAS using MEG in older adults, twenty-one with Parkinson's disease and twenty-three without. Everyone underwent high-density MEG during a beat-based cued tapping task, with rhythmic and non-rhythmic patterns. The data were analyzed with a Bayesian image reconstruction method, and a wavelet-based time-frequency decomposition was used to compute inter-trial phase locking to the auditory stimuli.

The key finding is that tapping with a rhythm, compared with a non-rhythmic sequence, produced different patterns of brain activity in each group. In healthy adults, there was greater activation of temporal, motor and parietal areas. In Parkinson's participants, there was greater reliance on parietal and frontal gyri.

Looking more closely, during rhythmic tapping, older adults without Parkinson's had significantly stronger neural activity in bilateral frontal, supplementary motor and primary motor areas. Conversely, those with Parkinson's showed significantly stronger activity in bilateral parietal regions, as well as the rolandic operculum and bilateral supramarginal gyri.

The interpretation: rhythmic auditory stimulation mobilizes diverse oscillatory networks. Healthy controls may shift to frontal areas, whereas patients with Parkinson's rely more on parietal areas, which may reflect frontal network dysfunction with compensation. The authors suggest these regions could serve as specific regions of interest for further RAS studies. It's a nice illustration of how MEG can inform not just diagnosis, but therapy mechanisms.

Part seven. Alterations of cortical structure and neurophysiology in Parkinson's disease are aligned with cortical neurochemical systems, by Wiesman and colleagues, with a preprint in 2023 and the published version in Annals of Neurology in 2024.

This study asks a deep question: how do the deviations in the cortex of Parkinson's patients relate to the brain's neurochemical systems, in a manner that corresponds to motor and cognitive symptoms?

The team measured cortical thickness from structural MRI, and spectral neurophysiological alterations from task-free MEG. They studied patients with idiopathic Parkinson's disease, seventy-nine for the MEG analysis and sixty-five for the MRI, and compared them with matched healthy controls, sixty-five for MEG and thirty-seven for MRI.

Using linear mixed-effects models and cortical atlases of nineteen neurochemical systems, they asked which receptor and transporter maps line up with the disease-related changes. They found that the structural and neurophysiological alterations align with several systems: acetylcholine, serotonin, glutamate and noradrenaline, albeit with different implications for motor and non-motor symptoms.

Some alignments appear protective. For example, the alignment of broadband power increases with acetylcholinergic systems is related to better attention function. By contrast, neurochemical alignment with structural alterations is associated with motor impairment, and alignment with other neurophysiological alterations is associated with psychiatric impairments.

The authors say this advances our understanding of how the nature of neurophysiological and structural cortical alterations relates to the characteristic symptoms of the disease, and they highlight the value of what they call a new nested atlas modeling approach for neurological and neuropsychiatric diseases. Notice the link back to part four: the cholinergic system, which responded to rivastigmine in Parkinson's dementia, shows up here as a key player, this time in protecting attention.

Part eight. Finally, the most recent paper, from 2026: Magnetoencephalography in Parkinson's disease, diagnostic potential and barriers to clinical translation, by Xia, Ding and Zhang.

This is a narrative review, and it brings us back to where we started. The authors begin by noting that Parkinson's disease is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder with motor and nonmotor manifestations. Diagnosis remains primarily clinical, and validated markers of early disease and subsequent progression are still needed.

MEG measures neuronal magnetic fields with millisecond temporal resolution, giving a way to characterize cortical dysfunction. The review critically synthesizes MEG evidence on periodic and aperiodic spectral activity, functional connectivity, network topology, and individual brain fingerprinting, with an emphasis on diagnostic relevance and barriers to translation.

Recurring findings include spectral slowing and cognitive-network disruption, consistent with what we heard in the Bosboom studies and the Olde Dubbelink study. But other measures, such as resting beta power, burst dynamics and coupling, depend on clinical phenotype, treatment state, and analytical choices. That echoes Stam's earlier point about medication.

The review is careful to distinguish evidence from EEG, functional MRI and invasive recordings from direct MEG findings. And on classification, it says that internally validated classification of established Parkinson's patients versus healthy controls is promising, but it does not establish performance in prodromal disease, or in clinically uncertain parkinsonism. That is where diagnosis is actually hard.

On the technology side, optically pumped magnetometers may improve recording flexibility, while still requiring control of environmental interference. And the authors call for prospective clinical cohorts, standardized acquisition and analysis, reproducibility studies, independent validation, and comparison with clinical assessment, all to establish incremental diagnostic and prognostic utility.

Let's pull everything together.

First, a consistent signature: across studies from 2005 to 2024, Parkinson's disease is associated with slowing of resting state brain activity. Dementia pushes that slowing further, into delta and alpha bands, and reduces reactivity to eye opening.

Second, connectivity matters. Longitudinal MEG shows early alpha one connectivity increases that later reverse, with alpha two decreases spreading across the cortex, and these changes track motor and cognitive decline.

Third, treatment and neurochemistry. Rivastigmine partly reverses the slowing, and the neurochemical atlas work links cholinergic and other systems to specific symptoms.

Fourth, function under task. During rhythmic tapping, patients appear to recruit parietal regions more, while healthy adults shift toward frontal and motor areas, suggesting compensation.

And fifth, the caveats. Early studies had samples of nine to forty-three patients. Medication state, phenotype, and analysis choices all change the results. And so the most recent review concludes that what's missing are large prospective cohorts, standardized methods, and independent validation, particularly for early or uncertain cases.

If you remember one sentence, make it this one: MEG can see Parkinson's disease as a slowing and disconnection of cortical rhythms that tracks symptoms and responds to treatment, but turning that into a reliable clinical test is still a work in progress.
